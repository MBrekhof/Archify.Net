P99 rose from 80 ms to 600 ms while the mean barely moved: a few requests got slow

Long image

A

Answer

A P99 spike with a flat mean means the slowest 1–2% of requests went from 80 ms to 600 ms while the rest stayed normal. The usual causes are queueing and contention, one bad instance or a slow downstream, or a few requests taking a slow path. Start the triage from the traces of slow requests, not from global metrics.

  1. The mean is blind to the tail: 15 of 1000 requests at 600 ms raise the mean by only about 8 ms.
  2. A worse tail almost always means one subset of requests has a problem, not the whole endpoint.
  3. The order: first find which segment is slow, then slice by dimension, then compare with changes and resources.

Applies toIllustrative numbersa typical web or microservice endpointP99 is the 990th of 1000 values

1

Why did the mean not move?

Take 985 requests at 80 ms plus 15 at 600 ms. The mean is (985×80 + 15×600) / 1000 = 87.8 ms, only 7.8 ms more. P99 takes the 990th value, which falls among the 15 slow requests, so it jumps to 600 ms. The mean hides tail problems, which is exactly why you monitor P95 and P99.

In what order do you triage?

  1. Confirm the metric ↗

    Check whether P50 and P95 rose too, and rule out a change in aggregation or sampling.

  2. Sample slow traces ↗

    Pull at least 10 traces of 500 ms or more; see whether the time goes to queueing, downstream calls or own processing.

  3. Slice by instance, downstream, parameter ↗

    Are the slow requests on one instance, one downstream, or do they share a parameter?

  4. Compare releases and config ↗

    Line the spike's start up with deploys, config changes, feature flags and dependency upgrades.

  5. Check resource contention ↗

    Look at connection pool waits, thread pool queues, GC pauses, CPU throttling and lock contention.
